Core theme
Self-awareness as the central discipline of leadership
Not self-optimization. Not performance hacks. The harder, slower work of actually knowing yourself — and what makes leading well possible in the first place.
Three lenses
The inner life
Parts, identity, psychological interior. IFS as the underlying framework.
The body
Somatic experience as a diagnostic tool — catching what the mind rationalizes away.
Power
Ethics of influence. A clinician's lens — power as something you're always already in.
The gap you fill
Leadership development has a follow-through problem. Everyone's read the books. The missing piece isn't more information — it's the interior work that makes any of it actually possible.
"Most leadership development tells you what to do. This is about why you don't — and what to do about that."
Your reader
Who they are: Leaders who've read the books, done the trainings, and still repeat the same patterns.
What they feel: A gap they've sensed but couldn't articulate.
What they think they need: A better system, stronger team, clearer strategy.
What they actually need: Interior work.
How to write for them
Open in their world
Lead with a relatable frustration or moment they'll recognize immediately.
Complicate the myth
Challenge what they believe: "good leaders stay calm," "self-awareness means knowing your strengths."
Take them somewhere new
Leave them with one thing to notice or try — and a door opened underneath it.
The leader's arc — the hidden framework behind every piece
Write from this, not about it. It's the infrastructure, not the content.
1
Lived experience creates a gap
Born into circumstances that give them things they don't need and withhold things they do. The wound precedes everything.
2
Exiles take the blame
The most "empowering" move available: self-blame. Exiled parts form core beliefs — "I'm unlovable," "I'm not enough." Shame becomes the organizing story.
3
Protective parts get to work
Parts develop strategies to get needs met — or the closest approximation available. Addiction, anxious attachment, performing, perfectionism, people-pleasing, control.
4
Parts-driven traits fuel the climb
Overambition, need for external validation, proving worth — these get leaders into leadership roles. The very traits driving them are protective parts running on old, painful fuel.
5
The effortfulness creates stuckness
Parts driving the bus, polarizations with parts that want to slow down or stop — the system creates blocks, exhaustion, burnout. Something stops working.
6
Stuckness met with more of the same
Thinking parts reach for more information — prescriptive books, trainings, frameworks. Self-critical parts push harder. This is where your reader finds you.
7
Negative cycles ensue
Varying degrees of success and failure, but the underlying system unchanged. The solution they're seeking is coming from the same parts that created the problem.
Writing prompt: What's a moment a leader would recognize — a pattern, a frustration, a stuck place — that is actually a part doing its job? Describe the surface experience with precision. Open a door underneath it.
Relationship to Brené Brown
Her work is descriptive and aspirational — extraordinary at naming dynamics, but prescriptive moves stay at the level of values and choices. The implicit model: once you understand and commit, you can do it.
Your distinction: knowing and doing are separated by something with its own logic — protective parts that won't stand down just because you've read a book. The reason you armored up is still living in your body.

Your bridge: "If Brown's work resonated but you've found it hard to actually change — this is why."
